The one-sentence version

You have a working conveyor belt with nothing on it: every moving part has been exercised end to end, and the only thing standing between this and a system that markets the studio is an API key — everything after that is optional and reversible.

Part one

Switching it on, in order

These are numbered because the order is a real dependency chain, not a preference. Each step is safe to stop at — the system keeps running in whatever state you leave it. Do not skip ahead to step 5.

1

Give it an API key

Not done · blocks everything

In the Supabase dashboard: Edge Functions → Secrets → Add new secret. Name it ANTHROPIC_API_KEY, paste the full sk-ant-… value from console.anthropic.com.

It belongs in Supabase because that is where the functions actually run. A key saved on your Mac, or in a .env file, does nothing — the code executing is on Supabase's servers, not yours. No redeploy is needed; the next run picks it up.

You'll know it worked when the System Health tab stops saying "Demo mode" and new drafts arrive without the [mock] tag. Give it up to a day, or ask me to trigger a run.

A note I'll keep repeating: this must be your key for your studio. When a client gets a system like this, they bring their own — never yours, or their usage lands on your bill and their access dies with your account.

2

Read the first real draft before connecting anything

Do this before step 3

Once the key is in, wait for one planning cycle and open the approval queue. Read what it writes about your studio, to your buyers, at your prices. This is the cheapest moment to discover it has the wrong idea about the business — nothing can reach anyone yet.

If the voice is off, the fix is not in the code. It is in the business context: the customer profiles, the service list, the proof points, and the writing rules. Those live in the database and every agent re-reads them on the next run. Tell me what is wrong and I will change the context, not the prompt.

You'll know it worked when you read a draft and think "I would actually post that" — or you can name exactly what is wrong with it. Both are useful. Neither is possible while it is writing placeholder.
3

Give approved posts somewhere to go

Nothing connected

Right now approving a post marks it approved and publishes it to a stub — a polite nowhere. Two routes out:

  • The webhook route (fast). One URL pointed at Make, Zapier, or n8n. That tool posts to Instagram, Facebook, LinkedIn, TikTok — anywhere it can already reach. No platform approval, no developer review, working the same afternoon.
  • The direct route (slower, sturdier). Facebook and Instagram through Meta's Graph API, LinkedIn through theirs. Both adapters are already written and waiting for credentials. Each needs a Meta or LinkedIn developer app, which takes days and a review.

Start with the webhook. It costs an afternoon and tells you whether publishing is even the bottleneck before you spend a week on API approval.

You'll know it worked when an approved post shows published with a real ID beside it instead of stub — and it is visibly on the platform.
4

Let it actually send email

Drafts only

Add SENDGRID_API_KEY and SENDGRID_FROM_EMAIL as Supabase secrets. Until then, follow-ups to new leads are written and stored, and no one receives them. There are six sitting there now.

This same key turns on alert emails from the System Health panel. Until it exists, problems appear on that page and nowhere else — the system cannot tell you something broke, it can only show you if you look.

Send from a separate domain if you ever do cold outreach. Warm replies to people who contacted you are fine on meridianinterface.com. Cold email is not: a spam complaint against your main domain can take your real client correspondence down with it.

You'll know it worked when a test lead you create receives the follow-up, and "Messages failed to send" on System Health stays at zero.
5

Decide how much rope it gets

Currently: you approve everything

One setting, two values. draft — where it is now — means the agent writes and you approve before anything reaches a person. auto means it sends follow-ups and publishes posts on its own.

My advice: stay on draft until you have approved thirty or so pieces and stopped being surprised by what it writes. Autonomy is worth having, but it is worth having after you know its habits — and it is one word to change, either direction, any time.

You'll know you're ready when your last several approvals were rubber stamps. If you are still editing, the system is not ready and switching to auto just publishes the edits you would have made.
Part two

How it runs once it's on

WhenWhat happensWhat you'd notice
Every 2 min Picks up queued work and does it — writing a draft, drafting a follow-up, publishing something you approved. Nothing. This is the part that should be invisible.
7:30am daily Measures the last 30 days: what was drafted, published, where leads actually came from, what it cost. Nothing directly — it changes what the 8:00am plan decides.
8:00am daily Reads the business context and yesterday's measurements, then decides the day's work: follow up new leads, draft content aimed at one customer profile. New drafts appear in your queue.
Mondays 8:30am Writes the weekly owner summary from the real numbers. A report you can read in two minutes.
Every 15 min Checks itself — schedules firing, work failing, sends failing, how long drafts have waited. Anything wrong appears on the System Health tab.

Times are Houston. The measurement step runs half an hour before the planner on purpose: the plan should be made by something that has already looked at what happened, not alongside it.

Part three

The part that is yours

There is exactly one human step in that diagram, and the system's own measurement has already flagged it. Its words, not mine:

"Nothing has ever been published. Do not plan more content volume. Nothing that has been written has reached an audience yet, so more drafts cannot teach us anything — the bottleneck is approval and publishing, not writing."

That is the honest shape of this. The system will happily write more than you can read. Drafting is free; your attention is not. Two consequences worth planning around:

  • Approve on a rhythm, not on inspiration. Ten minutes on a fixed day beats an hour when you happen to remember. The system flags a backlog once five items have waited a week — that alert exists because this is the predictable failure.
  • Rejecting is as useful as approving. An item you leave untouched teaches nothing and blocks the queue. Rejecting it is a decision the system can see.

And the measurement is honest in the other direction too. All four leads so far came from the website and a referral — none from anything the system published, because it has published nothing. It says so plainly rather than claiming credit for the website's work. That property is worth protecting: a marketing system that flatters itself is worse than none, because you would make real decisions on it.

Changed today

Two things that were wrong

Both were found by checking the running system rather than reading the code.

A

It was writing for the wrong business

Fixed and deployed

The placeholder copy was written for the events client this system was first built for. Every draft it produced for a web studio talked about "lighting, photo booths, and a DJ", and the lead follow-up asked web-design enquiries for their date and guest count. Nine such drafts were sitting in your approval queue.

Placeholder does not mean unread — you read it, and on auto a real lead would have received it. The copy now speaks as the studio and cites the published prices. The nine drafts are rejected with the reason recorded, not deleted.

B

A saved file didn't match what's running

Fixed

The health-check migration had been written from memory during a session that couldn't reach the database. It looked right and was wrong in four ways — different alert names, one shared staleness threshold instead of per-schedule ones, a different return type, and a three-day backlog window instead of seven. Applying it would have quietly downgraded the live checks and stranded every open alert.

It is now dumped from the live database and verified line-for-line against it. Worth stating the lesson plainly: a file that describes a running system is not evidence about that system. Only the system is.

Scope

What it deliberately does not do

  • It does not spend money on ads. Google, Meta and TikTok ad APIs are absent by choice, not oversight. Publishing a post costs nothing; an ads API spends your budget. That deserves its own decision and its own approval gate.
  • It does not promise Google rankings or invent clients, statistics, or testimonials. Those are written into its rules as hard prohibitions, alongside a ban on filler words like unlock, elevate, and seamless.
  • It does not claim credit it cannot prove. Leads from outside its channels are counted separately and labelled as not being marketing results.
