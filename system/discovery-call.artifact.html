Discovery Call

Seven questions, about twenty minutes, before you quote anything. They sort a business into one of four shapes and surface where its work actually breaks. Do not demo on this call.

The questions

Ask them in order. The first one does most of the work — let them talk through it without interrupting, and take notes rather than solving.

01
Walk me through the last customer you took on — from the first time you heard from them to the day you got paid.
Why
One open question sorts most businesses on its own. Everything after this only refines it.
Listen for
Was there a quote? A booking? Did something get shipped? Does it keep going after the invoice?
02
How do people reach you — and roughly how many a week?
Why
Volume decides whether a system pays for itself at all.
Listen for
More than one inbox. Phone and a form and Instagram DMs usually means the real problem is things get missed, not that anything is slow.
03
Between someone first contacting you and the work actually starting, what has to happen?
Why
This is what separates a quote-first business from an appointment business, and they need different plumbing.
Listen for
Site visits, measurements, approvals, deposits — and who is waiting on whom.
04
What do you or your staff type into one place after already typing it somewhere else?
Why
This is the sentence you quote back to them in the proposal. It names the waste in their own words.
Listen for
The same customer detail entered two or three times. Ask how long that takes a week — you want a number.
05
What are you paying for every month right now — and is it per person?
Why
The case for owning the system instead of renting it usually writes itself here.
Listen for
Per-seat pricing. A fee that grows every time they hire is the strongest argument you have, and it is theirs, not yours.
06
When something gets missed — an enquiry, a follow-up — what does that cost you?
Why
Without a number you cannot justify a price, and they will push back on it later instead of now.
Listen for
A real figure. A missed quote for a contractor is thousands; a missed haircut is sixty dollars. Those two businesses should not get the same proposal.
07
Who else touches this, and what happens when you are away?
Why
Decides logins, permissions and handover — and tells you who actually signs.
Listen for
“Only me.” A one-person business buys very differently from one with staff, and needs far less.

Sorting what you heard

By shape, not by industry. A dentist and a barber have nearly identical plumbing; a dentist and a hospital do not.

If they saidShapeThe spine you build
Every customer books a time; price is known up front appointment book → remind → visit → follow up → ask for a review
Every job is priced individually; there is a visit or a spec first quote-first enquire → spec → quote → approve → deposit → schedule
They sell items; something gets handed over or shipped order catalogue → order → pay → fulfil → buy again
The relationship continues; they bill monthly or per matter caseload intake → matter → recurring bill → status updates

The marketing layer is nearly the same on all four — that is the loop you already run. What differs is the operations layer underneath, which is why the second build of a shape is much faster than the first.

When not to sell it

Saying so on the call costs you one job and earns you the referral.

Under about five enquiries a week

Automation cannot pay for itself at that volume. Sell them a website that converts better, and revisit in a year.

They cannot say what a miss costs

Then they do not yet believe it costs anything, and every number you quote will feel expensive. Send them away to count for two weeks.

They want it to run with nobody watching

Have the approval conversation now, not at handover. A system that publishes unsupervised will eventually publish something they hate, and it will be your name on it.

Three people describe the process three different ways

The problem is the process, not the tools. Wiring it up will automate the disagreement. Offer the mapping work on its own first.

How to end it

One specific next step. No pressure, no package named yet.

“I’ll write up what I heard and what I’d build, with a price, in two working days. If I don’t think it’s worth the money, I’ll tell you that instead.”

Then do exactly that. The second sentence is what makes the first one credible, so it has to be true at least once — and the call where you talk someone out of spending is the one they tell other owners about.

Keep the filled-in notes. After three or four calls the same answers start repeating, and that is when a shape is worth turning into a reusable template — not before.
